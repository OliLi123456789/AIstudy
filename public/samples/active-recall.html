How to Study Effectively: Active Recall & Spaced Repetition

Why rereading feels good but doesn't work, how to quiz yourself instead, and how to time your reviews against the forgetting curve.

Most students study by rereading notes and highlighting. It feels productive — the material becomes familiar — but familiarity is not the same as being able to recall it in an exam. Decades of research on memory point to two techniques that consistently outperform rereading: active recall and spaced repetition.

The Forgetting Curve

In the 1880s, psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how quickly newly learned information fades. He found a predictable pattern: without review, most forgetting happens within the first day, then continues more slowly. The curve flattens with each successful review — each well-timed repetition pushes the memory back up and makes it decay more slowly next time.

Why Rereading Fails

  • Recognition is not recall. Rereading makes the material look familiar, which tricks you into thinking you know it.
  • It's passive. No effort is required to retrieve the information, so no strong memory is formed.
  • It wastes time. Hours of rereading produce far less retention than a short self-quiz.

Active Recall: Quiz Yourself

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory rather than putting it back in front of your eyes. Every retrieval strengthens the memory and exposes gaps. Practical ways to do it:

  • Close the book and write everything you remember about a topic, then check what you missed.
  • Use flashcards — read the question, answer aloud, then flip.
  • Do practice questions and past papers without looking at notes first.
  • Explain the topic to someone else (or an empty room) from memory.
💡 Rule of thumb: if answering a question doesn't require effort, you're probably recognizing, not recalling. Struggle is a feature — it's the signal that a memory is being strengthened.

Spaced Repetition: Time Your Reviews

Spacing spreads reviews across increasing intervals, scheduling each one just before you would forget. A simple schedule looks like this: review on day 1, day 3, day 7, day 21. Each time you answer correctly, the interval grows; each time you fail, it shrinks. Modern apps (including AIstudy) automate this with algorithms that model your personal forgetting curve.

A 30-Minute Study Session Template

  1. 5 min: Skim the material and decide what matters.
  2. 15 min: Active recall — write or answer from memory.
  3. 5 min: Check against the source and fix gaps.
  4. 5 min: Note what you forgot; schedule the next review.

Flashcards

What is active recall?

Pulling information out of memory (e.g. self-quizzing) rather than passively rereading it.

What is the forgetting curve?

The pattern in which newly learned information fades quickly at first, then more slowly, without review.

Why does rereading feel effective?

It creates familiarity — recognition — which feels like knowledge even when recall would fail.

What is spaced repetition?

Reviewing material at increasing intervals, each timed just before you would forget it.

What happens to the forgetting curve after each successful review?

The memory decays more slowly each time, so intervals between reviews can grow.

Name one active recall technique.

Writing everything you remember from memory, using flashcards, doing practice questions, or explaining a topic aloud.

Quiz yourself

Recognition and recall are equally good evidence that you know something. True or false?

False. Recognition is much easier than recall; only recall reliably predicts exam performance.

Without review, when does most forgetting happen?

Within the first day, after which forgetting slows.

Which schedule follows the principle of spaced repetition?

Reviewing on days 1, 3, 7 and 21 — intervals grow over time.

If you keep answering a flashcard correctly, what should happen to its review interval?

It should grow — correct recalls push the next review further into the future.
