How Memory Works: The Forgetting Curve

In the 1880s, a German psychologist measured his own forgetting — and produced a curve that still explains why your exam prep works (or doesn't) today.

The experiment

Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables ("ZOF", "WID", "KAF") and tested himself at intervals, recording how much he could still recall. The result: memory decays fast at first, then slower. Roughly half of what he learned was gone within a day; what survived the first few days decayed much more slowly.

What the curve means for you

  • The first 24 hours decide everything. Most forgetting happens immediately. A single review on day 1 can double what survives the week.
  • Forgetting is normal, not failure. The curve applies to everyone. What separates strong students is scheduled contact with the material.
  • Each review flattens the curve. The memory comes back faster, lasts longer, and needs less review next time. This is why spaced repetition works.

How to use it

Treat learning as a series of well-timed touches: learn → review within 24 hours → review again on day 3 → day 7 → day 21. Each touch should be active recall, not rereading. Sleep also matters: memory consolidation happens during sleep, so the all-nighter directly sabotages the very process you're relying on.

🛌 The cheapest memory hack: study in the evening and review the next morning. Sleep consolidates the first pass, and the morning review catches it before the steep part of the curve.
