How to Read a Textbook: The SQ3R Method

Reading a chapter once with intention beats rereading it five times on autopilot. SQ3R turns passive reading into an active system.

Why rereading textbooks fails

Rereading feels like studying because the material becomes familiar — but familiarity is recognition, not recall. Worse, rereading has no failure signal: you never find out what you don't know, so you keep rereading everything. SQ3R fixes both problems by making every pass do a specific job.

The five steps

  1. Survey (5 minutes). Before reading a word: skim the headings, bold terms, figures, and the chapter summary. You're building a mental map, not learning details. The questions from this step matter more than the reading.
  2. Question. Turn every heading into a question. "The Water Cycle" becomes "How does water move through the cycle?" Now the chapter is a list of questions you're trying to answer — reading with purpose instead of drifting.
  3. Read (actively). Read one section at a time, looking for the answer to that section's question. Underline sparingly — mark only what answers the question. If you can't find the answer, that's a flag: reread the section once, then move on and mark it for follow-up.
  4. Recite. Close the book. Answer the section's question out loud, in your own words. This single step — retrieval — does more for retention than everything else combined.
  5. Review. At the end of the chapter, answer every section question from memory. What you can't answer becomes tomorrow's review list, scheduled with spaced repetition.
📖 The 80/20 of chapters: introduction, summary, and headings carry most of the structure. Read the middle for explanations, not for every word. If a paragraph doesn't answer one of your questions, it's skimmable.

From chapter to study material

The section questions you wrote are your flashcards. Put the question on one side and your own answer on the other, then review on a schedule. For dense or technical chapters, this is also exactly the conversion AIstudy performs automatically: import the chapter and it produces notes, flashcards and a quiz from the same content — but you'll retain far more if you attempt the conversion yourself first, then use the generated material to check and fill gaps.

Common mistakes

  • Highlighting first, thinking later. Mark only after you can say why a sentence matters.
  • Reading end-to-end without stopping. One section at a time, with a recite between each.
  • Skipping the survey. Five minutes of mapping saves twenty of floundering.
