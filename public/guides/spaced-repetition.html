A Beginner's Guide to Spaced Repetition

Review each fact just before you'd forget it, and the gap before the next review grows. It's the single most efficient way to build long-term memory — and the exact opposite of cramming.

The idea in one sentence

Memories fade on a predictable curve — fast at first, then slower. Spaced repetition reviews each item at expanding intervals, timed so each review happens right before the memory would fade. Every successful retrieval stretches the interval further.

Why cramming loses

Cramming shoves everything into short-term memory in one night. It can scrape you through tomorrow's test, but almost everything is gone within days. Spaced repetition spreads the same total time across a month and produces memory that survives the semester. The research is unambiguous: spacing is one of the largest, most replicated effects in learning science.

A schedule you can start today

  1. Day 0: learn the material once (see our active recall guide).
  2. Day 1: first review — the most important one. Test yourself on everything.
  3. Day 3: review again; mark what you failed.
  4. Day 7: third pass, focused on the weak items plus a light sweep.
  5. Day 21: final pass before the exam window.

If you miss an item, its interval resets to short. If you get it right, the interval grows. Over time the schedule shapes itself around your memory, not the calendar.

Pitfalls

  • Self-grading generously. "I basically knew it" doesn't count. If you didn't produce the answer, reset the interval.
  • Reviewing everything equally. Once an item is solid, let its interval stretch — don't waste reviews on what you already know.
  • Skipping the day-1 review. The first review determines whether anything survives at all.
