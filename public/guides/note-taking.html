How to Take Notes That Help You Learn

Notes are not a recording of the lecture — they're a tool for thinking. The best notes force you to process information, not just transcribe it.

Why verbatim notes fail

Typing or writing every word a lecturer says feels productive but teaches little. Verbatim transcription uses your hands while your brain idles. Studies comparing verbatim notetakers with students who summarized in their own words consistently find the summarizers remember and understand more. The processing is the learning: choosing what matters, rewording it, and connecting it to what you already know is where the memory gets built.

Two methods that work

The outline method

  • Main topics at the left, indented details beneath.
  • One idea per line, in your own words.
  • Gaps in the outline are gaps in understanding — mark them with a "?" and chase them later.

The Cornell method

Split the page: a narrow left column for cues and questions, a wide right column for notes, and a bottom strip for a summary. After class, write a question in the left column for every chunk of notes on the right — then cover the right side and answer the questions from memory. Cornell turns note-taking into built-in active recall.

📝 Golden rule: after every session, close the notes and write a three-sentence summary from memory. If you can't, you've found today's weakest point.

Digital or paper?

Both work; what matters is the processing, not the medium. Digital notes are searchable and editable; handwritten notes force summarization by sheer slowness. If you type, summarize harder. If you write, review sooner.

Turn notes into review material

Notes are raw material, not the study plan. The last step is conversion: turn each section into questions (or let AIstudy generate flashcards and quizzes from your notes automatically), then review with spaced repetition. A note you never re-encounter is just decoration.
